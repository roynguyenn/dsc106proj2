Proposition: After the NYPD’s body-camera pilot program rollout in 2014, male police officers received a higher number of substantiated civilian complaints compared to their female counterparts.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • 1.5, Baseline of counts starts at 2014 to emphasize the change after body-cam rollouts. It faithfully shows the trend of counts after 2014.
  • 1.5, Added red vertical line at 2019 to highlight the difference at the latest year. It just shows the difference between the 2019 year.
  • -0.5, Annotated the height of female and male officer counts that emphasizes its difference. It contains some language that has bias in it to sway the reader's mind.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • -1.5, Decided to use substantiation rate. This is pretty deceptive since there's a large difference between female and male officers to begin with and having less data points leads to more variation.
  • 1, Reducing the opacity of the pre-2014 bars. It deceives the reader by diverting their attention to the post 2014 rates.
  • -1.5, Deciding to group on pre and post 2014. There are way less female officer data than there is male, and grouping post 2014 would further limit the amount of female officer data that got transformed.

Final Reflection

Throughout designing my graphs, the most straightforward part was creating a time-series plot that counted the number of substantiated complaints against male and female officers. I was able to clearly highlight a trend that differentiates male and female gender officers because the data naturally showed this pattern. However, the most difficult part was showing an opposite trend. I had to brainstorm a lot and come up with different metrics that would persuade the reader of the opposite trend. Here, I ultimately choose to work with substantiation rates, which may seem earnest initially. Behind the scenes, however, the reason why it showed the opposite trend was due to the lack of female officer data inflating their proportions. What suprised me was how easy it was to create a graph that misleds the reader, making me question if half or more of the visualizations I see online had data similarly manipulated to show a different biased trends. This leads into how I define ethical analysis and visualization. First and foremost, if we are creating a visualization, there should be enough datapoints to support what the intended takeaway. Although "enough" might seem subjective, I would say around 100 or more observations would be sufficient in most contexts. Another aspect about designs to point out are annotations and title making. I believe having neutral language is the most ethnical, yet in my graphs, I used strongly biased language like "startling" and "staggering" that shows bias torwards certain trends. Additionally, being transparent with the reader about design choice and biases are essential for ethical visualizations. If the language and tone seem biased but the numbers support what the message is conveying, then its more so persuasive than misleading. For instance, I had a biased title that depicted male officers having higher substantiation complaints, and the data legitimately supported that claim. Drawing the line between acceptable, persuaive choices and misleading ones ultimately depends on how much the designer wants to communicate their message. Generally, a visualizaion is ethical when they are transparent with their design decisions, even if it is blatently persuasive. However, when a visualization intentionally hides their data transformations or distorts the data to convey a certain narrative, it is more so unethical and misleading.
